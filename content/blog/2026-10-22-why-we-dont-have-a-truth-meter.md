An article gets passed through a machine. A colourful dial appears: **83% true**. It feels decisive. It may also mean almost nothing.

Did the system check independent sources, or did it count ten outlets repeating one press release? Did it examine the wording of a factual claim, or simply judge the author's tone? Was there a verified primary document? Did it test an alleged cause against an alternative explanation?

A score without answers to those questions is often a decoration pretending to be an instrument.

## The claim is not the evidence

Consider a fictional article alleging that a policy increased investment. That sentence contains a causal assertion. The article may quote a minister and a business association, but two favourable quotations do not independently establish the effect.

An audit needs distinct records:

- **The assertion:** what would have to be true, in which country and over what years?
- **The presented support:** which figures, documents or witnesses does the article mention?
- **The independent checks:** what do sources outside that article establish?
- **The method:** which comparisons would separate correlation from causation?
- **The uncertainty:** what relevant data or assumptions are still missing?

Calling each quotation an independent confirmation makes the graph look impressively connected while leaving the central causal question unanswered.

## Bias and falsehood are different questions

A journalist can accurately report a budget figure yet choose a selectively flattering comparison. Another can be openly opinionated and still report its individual factual assertions correctly.

We therefore refuse to collapse **accuracy**, **selection**, **framing** and **values** into one political correctness meter.

A challengeable report should show the original proposition, quoted evidence, source links, alternative interpretations and the reason a conclusion might change.

## There is no privileged fact-checker

That includes us. Our source selection can be incomplete. Our rules can be wrong. Our AI models can misread an article or invent an attribution.

The first analysis tool extracts arguments and hypotheses; its output is labelled as **AI-generated and not independently verified**. The next layers will need provenance checks and a reviewable history of challenges before they can responsibly offer stronger conclusions.

A reader should be able to click a claim and ask: *What exactly supports this?* If the answer is unavailable, the interface should say so.

## Test the method in public

The [open methodology](/methodology) is a working protocol, not scripture. Its current version and source code can be examined, criticised and revised.

A better standard of public reasoning starts by making it easy to prove the system wrong. That is considerably more useful than building a prettier dial.
