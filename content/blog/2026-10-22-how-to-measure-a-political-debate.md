Who received the most time in a debate? Who interrupted whom? Did the moderator challenge every candidate equally?

Those are empirical questions worth studying. They are also remarkably easy to answer badly.

Imagine a round-table programme with a moderator and four guests. A chart announces that one guest got the **least airtime**. Unless we can inspect the recording, timings and counting rules, that chart is an assertion rather than an audit.

## First decide what counts as speaking time

Is the clock running during cross-talk? Does a guest's microphone being audible mean they have the floor? Are prerecorded clips included? Should a moderator's summary of a participant's position count toward that participant?

We need definitions before the numbers:

1. **Audio provenance.** Show the original recording URL, broadcast date, transcript version and any edits.
2. **Speaker identification.** Attach speaker IDs to time intervals and explain how diarisation errors were corrected.
3. **Airtime.** Measure the union of intervals spoken by each person; disclose whether overlapping speech counts for both.
4. **Interruptions.** Distinguish genuine floor-taking from agreement, simultaneous starts and supportive interjections.
5. **Moderator treatment.** Count and classify questions, follow-ups, cut-offs and available response time.
6. **Uncertainty.** Allow the public to contest a specific timestamp, speaker label or rule.

## Even a perfect stopwatch cannot settle fairness

Suppose one participant speaks for fewer minutes because they choose concise answers. Another receives a longer answer window only after making an unusually complex factual assertion. Equal speaking time would not automatically mean equal treatment, nor would unequal speaking time automatically prove bias.

A serious analysis should compare several possible explanations. It should not conclude intent merely from a pattern.

The visual summary ought to have a direct route back to the underlying timestamped transcript. A challenge such as *“This was an overlap, not an interruption”* should create a reviewable correction, not disappear under an opaque score.

## Compare debates across languages and formats

The same method could be applied to a municipal meeting, a parliamentary hearing, a televised election debate or a public scientific discussion. But the context must travel with the figures.

Translated transcripts, remote connections, microphones and edited video can all affect measurements. An AI diarisation model helps produce a first pass; it does not become an authoritative witness.

## What exists today, and what comes next

Our article-analysis workspace is an early **text-only** implementation. It does not yet perform audio diarisation, verified speaking-time measurements or automatic debate scoring.

That distinction is important. We can publish the proposed [methodology](/methodology) now, accept criticism, and build audiovisual measurement as a separate evidence-backed module. We should only publish measured debate results when the source recording, algorithm and correction process can be inspected.

The goal is not to crown a winner. It is to make it harder for anyone—including us—to hide how the result was produced.
